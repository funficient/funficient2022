Human-Centered Requirements: Why Relationships Matter More Than Jira

A case for a more human centered approach to eliciting requirements.

By Kate Dames · June 17, 2017

Bad requirements are born when people stop talking to each other.

I was born in an age with little technology. TV officially started its first broadcast in our country a few months prior to my birth, and the internet only became a thing when I went to univerity. While the majority of people only embraced the concept of a personal computer a decade or more later, in our household, technology has always been present. With an Aquarian father, we were one of the first households in town to have a personal computer, and from a young age my father's love language was showing me how the technology works and all the possibilities that comes with it.

It thus made sense that I decided to study computer science and landed my first job testing systems. In those days, though, systems were an elite offer only available to a handful of companies. There were hardly any tools to manage requirements or workflows. You tested software by using it. And you built what those elite users asked for by interacting with them.

There weren't separate roles for different functions either, dividing the work into different parts. The person who interacted with the customer was the one responsible for resolving issues and defining and translating needs. Usually using conversations, and occasionally following up with an email if it was too complex to remember. Requirements management was merely seen as a necessary feedback loop as part of the development process.



Requirements Gathering Before Agile and Jira

It made sense and it worked. The person who became aware of the customer need was the one to raise it with the team of developers. We didn’t need processes or tools to drive decision making and work. We all had common sense, clear goals, and a shared desire to do a good job. We all felt responsible for the success of the project and spoke to each other daily without ever needing to call a meeting or put an agenda together. Partly because we wanted to do a good job, and also because we liked talking to each other.

And if, by any chance, you think the systems then weren't as complex as today, think again. We had a fully integrated system linking different departments and vendors consisting of different systems and hardware, into a seamless user experience.

From the moment a customer swiped their loyalty card to get free parking on the casino grounds, to ordering food and drinks from one of the independent hotel restaurants, to playing on the slot machines, to cashing out and getting their winnings, it was all integrated and seamless. Each and every event on a slot machine was monitored and recorded in the system; there were "progressive" jackpots where slot machines manufactured by different vendors were all linked up to a single jackpot, and each time a door to a slot machine was opened or a coin inserted, there was an event registered that allowed the accounting department to get automated reports. Whenever there were a discrepancy in the sequence of triggers or an event that didn't match the expected pattern, an alert was triggered to the security department and surveillance cameras, also fully integrated into the system. Even table games without any technology, like poker and roulette, were monitored and linked to the loyalty system, so that customers could earn points for playing there as well which they could spend anywhere on the casino floors, from ordering a drink to booking a hotel room.

It was a complex system, with technology far ahead of its time. The smart cards we today use for everything were just being introduced then, and integrated systems that were connected to a head office in a different town were practically unheard of. Most systems were standalone, and there were no communication outside the physical network.

At the time I didn't realize how lucky I was to be at the forefront of such a complex technology project, and assumed it was just the way things were done. What made this experience even more special and extraordinary was that the gaming culture was more like a close-knit family than a corporate environment. We were all passionate about what we were doing, we were serious about work, and as serious about play, and we did everything together due to the nature of the work, which meant we spent months on end together in isolated small towns where we lived and worked together based on what needed to be done, rather than what we were told we should do.



Building More Than Software: Trust, Camaraderie, and Customer Focus

Once of the most memorable projects were when we installed a new casino at a small and very distant seaside town. We arrived according to plan, but the project was delayed and the decision was made that it would be more cost effective to stay there than fly back and forth. So for a month or two we spent our days jet skiing, playing tennis, and going out to dinner at night, not doing much work at all. However, the moment the network was ready and we could start with the system implementation, we were all hands on deck and worked around the clock to get everything done. When, totally exhausted after a 16 hour shift, we would take a 15 minute nap under a table, or go to the on-site apartments to shower, only to come back immediately and continue working. We played tricks on each other, lost our tempers, and were seen at our absolute worst without any judgement. It was as human an experience as it gets, and we all had so much fun doing it.

After such an intense project we didn't take leave, we simply came back to head office for a few days to wind down in the office. We couldn’t wait to get together to "save the world" with technology. No problem was too big and no customer requirement not taken seriously. We never considered that the customer asked for something that they didn't truly need, and went out of our way to ensure that the software met their actual needs. Sometimes it meant saying no to what they asked for, sometimes it meant spending time training them how to use the system, but mostly it meant translating the needs into requirements that could be implemented by the developers. Seeing the impact of our work first hand was more motivating than any badge, leader board, or good performance rating. Knowing that you were responsible for solving a customer’s problem, were meaningful, and we felt like hero’s most days.



When Requirements Was Built on Relationships, Not Tools

The first system I was involved in was a systems development project that was deemed as impossible. After months of negotiating, head office finally agreed to allocate two engineers for three months to keep our rather persuasive startup owners quiet. They didn't think it was possible, but I was young and inexperienced and didn't know any better. I assumed it's possible, without any baseline or prior experience to limit my beliefs.

We had 3 months and 3 people to deliver a fully integrated and compliant casino monitoring and controlling system using new software and hardware never used before. The control system was developed in Australia, the hardware from all over the globe, all with different standards and formats, and the firmware linking the hardware to the system a brand new technology developed in Austria. It was as global and as complex as projects get.

The next 3 months we spent mainly chatting to each other and soon became friends. The genius Australian front-end developer and the Iranian (or was he Iraqi, I always got it wrong which fueled a passionate but friendly discussion) who handled the firmware and hardware integration, and the South African putting everything together and testing it against the detailed international standards.

We came from diverse backgrounds and had different interests and we cared little about what nationality we were or which pronouns we preferred, or our political and religious views. All we cared about was building something awesome and solving a problem that everyone thought couldn’t be done.

We spent more time talking than working, curious to get to know more about each other's cultures and countries. But somewhere between these conversations we spent a few hours working each day and 90 days later we successfully shipped. We easily made the impossible possible without any stress or overtime and soon the product was released worldwide and eventually became the flagship product of the company.



Reality Strikes

Fast forward. The system is running in a casino for the first time in the world and I learn the hard lesson about the difference between theory and practice. To my dismay I’m the only one who knows how the system works and able to support it, which ends up being needed every 5 minutes due to some new issue that is discovered. What worked perfectly well in the lab suddenly was a top priority issue on the floor. I recall a period where for months I literally only went home once every 24–48 hours for a quick shower and maybe get a few minutes of sleep before the next urgent phone call woke me.

After this experience I never looked at documented requirements again, especially highly legislated ones, realizing how worthless they were compared to real life.

Integration can only happen when you’re connected

Looking back I’m still amazed at what we did in such a short time though.

Today, with the majority of society having permanent access to high-speed internet and spoiled for choice when it comes to any application, it is hard to find an integrated experience like that, even with the help of Artificial Intelligence. Just try to contact the bank with a problem on your account or make an insurance claim that doesn't fit the 'standard' moldto find out exactly how disconnected the world is. It always seems to be someone else’s fault, or a different department’s responsibility.



Where did it all go wrong?

Comparing requirements management then and now, here is a list of the reasons how bad requirements are born and how to prevent it.

Lesson 1: Relationships matter more than processes and tools

Requirements are all about conversations. That’s why they call it user stories.

We spent more time chatting than anything else those 3 months, yet, we did more work than the rest of the company combined. What was done was done right with no need to redo anything, because we understood what was needed and why. We were aligned, and laser zoom focused on the goal.

We spent plenty of time understanding the need, which meant it only took a few minutes to get the work done as the developers knew exactly what to do. I could in most cases give feedback within a few hours, so there were no delays and no need to log a Jira ticket. If it was important, it was done immediately.

Agile didn’t exist yet, but this was better than agile. No tools, no rules. Only responsible people who cared.

The work was done quickly and correctly because we loved solving problems, wanted to help each other and understood what needed to be done. The engineers understood the requirements and I understood the constraints and big picture, and together we easily problem-solved.

All of this would never have happend if we had a manager constantly bugging us to find out how we’re doing and how long it will take or having to update a workflow system.

A requirements management system effectively stops the conversations from happening.

If you don’t want to drink a beer or grab a coffee with the people you work with, you’re in the wrong team.

When people don’t like each other, errors are introduced to the software and bad requirements are born and built. Building relationships is the most important tool that any product owner can have. All the fancy features and reports in Jira or the real-time access of Trello doesn’t get close to a 5 minute chat with the team.

Get rid of the tools and start building relationships.



Lesson 2: Requirements are stories to be talked over, not written down

I was surprised when I realized that what worked in the isolated lab environment didn’t work in real life.

After, however, spending close to 24 hours per day on site he first few weeks after launch to sort out these birthing hiccups, I understood why. I never once spoke to a customer in the lab, only relying on the legal requirements and the engineer’s skills, who hasn't spoken to a customer either.

In the live environment, I had to spend a lot of time crawling on the floor between the cables and talking with the slots technicians between 2 and 4 am in the morning as this was the quiet period. And I wasn't paid overtime. But I loved it. It felt connected and it felt real. I could experience the impact of my work and failures first hand, and that made it meaningful.

I was never too busy to engage in conversation, partly because I didn’t have to go and update a workflow management system, partly because I thrived in being able to solve people’s issues. I felt like wonder woman. It was great.

During these mostly informal interactions I received more insights into what is important and what not than any amount of documentation or Jira ticket ever provided. And when there was a need, it was done within minutes.

There was never a need to create a backlog. Either it was important or not. If it was important, it was done. Immediately. You only need a to write something down when you can’t remember the details anymore.

You don't need a backlog, you need priorities.

Through my conversations I also discovered that it’s not only the punters that use the system and the IT team that serve them. The slots technicians, the PR team, the waiters and the surveillance staff all use it much more than the players, and their needs directly affect the ability to serve the paying customers and the user experience.

I learned that error code 87672x000 means nothing to anyone, including the developers who wrote it. I learned that accounting is useless if there is not a financial report to interpret the information. I learned that even if the information on the report is perfect but it can’t be printed or is not available when you need it, it’s useless. I learned that a customer doesn’t know the difference between a Bally or an Atronic or an IGT slot machine or where the machine stops and where the system starts, and they shouldn’t either. All they cared about was getting their payout and having a good experience.

The core role of the product owner is to spend time with the customers to understand their needs. The real users. Not the ones selling it or paying for it, but the ones actually using it. If you’re not getting your hands dirty and dealing with customer complaints, you are not doing your job as product owner.

Bad requirements are born when you don’t understand who actually uses the system and what they need.



Lesson 3: Focus on the goal

One day, I was struggling with a very serious problem involving jackpot payouts. Every time a jackpot was hit all the servers had to be restarted, rendering the casino off-line for a few minutes. During peak hours, this was every few minutes. I learned first hand what a blocking issue is.

The IT manager did what managers tend to do in a crisis. He kept blaming and nagging, wanting to know why we didn’t pick it up earlier and when it’s going to be fixed. He had right to be upset because it was a very serious issue, but we were there within minutes from the time it was reported, spent the next 48 hours without any sleep troubleshooting, immediately flew an engineer from Austria out to assist us and had Australia on the line literally 24/7 trying to resolve the issue. We literally couldn't do more.

He wasn't helping, and making it hard to get any work done. And after weeks of little to no sleep, I lost my cool and, in front of everyone told him to back off. In rather more harsh words than that.

But I was tired, and continued to explain to him why he was being an asshole and that I was doing my best and that he wasn’t helping. I also explained that we couldn’t have done anything more and there was no way to have prevented the issue. The only way to test a jackpot is either by emulating it, which we did with every manufacturer, or spend months playing a real machine from each manufacturer hoping for the 1 in 16 million chance that it will hit, which would have meant that their project would have been delayed by a year and cost at least ten times more.

Shocked by someone daring to question his authority, he listened to every word, not saying a word. He realized I was right and that he was being unreasonable. He left us alone and within a few hours the problem was solved.

At first my colleagues were shocked as you don’t speak to a customer like that, especially if it’s the IT manager, effectively the most important person in the casino. Even if they’re wrong. I am however not like most people. I am not impressed by titles, and whether you’re the CEO or the cleaner, if you’re being unreasonable and prevent me from doing my work, I’m going to tell it to you. You earn my respect by being good at your job, and how you treat people, not the title you wear.

Our relationship after this encounter was better than ever. There was a new level of respect that wasn’t there before. But I never again relied on emulations or harnesses to verify whether something works or not. I only ever tested the real end-to-end workflow.

Bad requirements are born when you care more about keeping your manager happy than focusing on the actual problem.

Equally, bad requirements are built when you rely on theory, not reality. Labs are not real.



Lesson 4: Understand the problem before attempting to solve it

In most cases, what the customer wants is already in the system, they just need someone to show them how to use it or where to find it. It’s the product owner’s job to understand why customers are asking for what they are asking before just reacting and putting it on a backlog.

Usually, what they are asking for is not what they want or what they even need. They are merely trying to express a need within their world view. They might ask for a spreadsheet, when in fact they want information to make a decision. When you say yes to everything, you don’t understand the value of why it is being developed.

The primary role of the product owner is to add value.

What benefit does this new enhancement have? What is the impact of implementing it? Is it really useful to the actual users?

If you don’t spend time with the customer where they show you why they need something, it is not possible for you to understand their need.



Lesson 5: Get rid of formalized processes and hierarchy

The best work I did was in the most informal settings. During our 90 day challenge, we didn’t have any daily standups or formal processes.

Here’s the thing. No one wants to spend their days updating Jira tickets or attending meetings. People want to feel like a hero saving the day with their skills. Managing requirements provides a false sense of security for a manager. It doesn't create value. Your product is as valuable as the problems it solves, not the number of tickets you close.

Conversations should happen in realtime, not when there’s a spot available in your calendar.

When I wasn’t clear on something, I walked to the developer. Actually, I spent more time with the developers in the lab than at my desk. When the developers were unsure about something, they would ask me. When the customers needed something, they phoned and I immediately responded. They knew who to phone and that their problem would be solved. There was trust. Everyone was happy. The developers, the managers, and the customers.

But then things started to change. Maybe it was because I didn't realize how burnt out I was after three years without a holiday. Maybe it was the extra management layer that was added to our team that caused the manager to feel excluded and insecurre. He was a nice guy, but constantly reminded us how important management is now that the company is growing. We sort of believed him. For a while. Irony is that the only growth in the company was the addition of him and a marketing manager.

We attended his kids' birthday parties and liked each other, but the team who could do anything, started to deteriorate. We started arguing, and started shifting responsibilities. He did everything managers do. First, he introduced a weekly team meeting and a monthly meeting with the customers. I was however considered too junior and was no longer allowed to speak directly to the customers. He "promoted" his personal friend to become the customer proxy and even though we all loved her, she wasn't technical and didn't understand anything about networks, which was typically what the customer meetings was about. When the word database or operating system popped up, her beautiful blue eyes would cross with confusion. She was however a very good trainer for the casino floor staff, so she was very valuable in the team as no-one else wanted to do that. But she wasn't able to understand the needs of the customers with her limited technical knowledge. Customer service started declining as more and more requirements were misunderstood or unnecessary due to the new process that was introduced by the new manager.

The customers started complaining, the team started bickering. Our wings were clipped, unable to fly.

We now had a schedule to be on-call for customers and had to be available at certain times - apparently in our best interest. The customers couldn’t phone us directly anymore and the team started arguing. Previously it was a non-issue with everyone always being available because they loved being needed by the customers.

We started having performance appraisals where we were told where we’re not good enough and everyone hated it but no-one said anything, silenced by the constant reminder of how this was better for the company. In truth however, that’s what killed the culture and was far from good for anyone, including the company.

Then, one day, the gaming board phoned and asked for me. I spent an hour on the phone patiently explaining something and fast-tracked the slow and expenesive certification process as a result. My manager was furious. He didn't appreciate that the prestigeous gaming board spoke to the most junior team member rather than to him. To him, the hierarchy was more important than the work. He didn't have information the gaming board needed, and I was reprimanded for not following the process he so carefully designed that says outlined a communication plan.

My passion was killed instantly and I was no longer motivated to spend weekends at work or do anything more than what was required. The hierarchy and processes killed my ability to service our customers. I hated it. I hated not being able to connect directly with the customers anymore. I hated not being able to solve problems and use my talent productively.

Where previously I felt I was making a difference, now I felt caged in. I finally resigned in search of a workplace where I could focus on work rather than participate in political games.

Bad requirements are born when you care more about the roles and hierarchy of control than the the impact of your work.



Conclusion

Good requirements naturally happen when there are good relationships. The more control you try to impose, the less natural a relationship is, the worse the requirements become, the more error-prone and disconnected the system gets.

If you want to be really agile, throw away the tools and the processes and focus on the relationships in your team and the work.
